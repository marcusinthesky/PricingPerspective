\section*{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A firm's economic peers matter for benchmarking, risk measurement, and the
estimation of return dependence, yet the economically relevant peer of a
diversified firm need not be another individual firm.
Industry membership, supply-chain links, geographic proximity, and shared news
coverage each provide economically meaningful definitions of relatedness.
In spatial finance, such relations determine the interaction structure through
which firms' returns are connected
\citep{fernandez_spatial_2011,kou_asset_2018,ge_news-implied_2023}.
Our starting point is that the relevant peer can instead be a weighted
combination of firms, each representing a different part of the target's
economic activities.

A diversified firm illustrates the distinction.
One candidate may resemble its technology business, another its customer base,
and another its production or supply-chain environment.
No individual firm need closely resemble the whole.
Pairwise similarity ranks each candidate separately; joint representation asks
which combination best represents the target and how much each member
contributes.
A firm can therefore be an important peer even when it is not especially close
to the target on average.
Its relevance depends on what it contributes alongside the other members of the
basket.

Language models make this compositional peer structure measurable from firms'
information environments.
A text embedding model maps each article to a fixed-dimensional vector, with
semantically related texts placed nearby in the resulting representation space.
This geometry, commonly used for semantic retrieval, lets a collection of
articles be treated as a distribution of economic information.
A firm's articles therefore form a distribution of positions, which we call its
information footprint.
Two firms can have the same average position while occupying different
footprints, much as retail chains with the same average store location can
serve different regions.
We use Wasserstein geometry to align each candidate peer's articles with those
of the target, then choose the nonnegative weights, summing to one and
excluding the target, that best reconstruct the target from the aligned peer
footprints.
This target-anchored barycentric reconstruction produces a customized
information-peer basket for every firm.
Across firms, these baskets form a directed map $W^\flat$ of a latent
information field: firm $j$ can contribute strongly to firm $i$'s basket while
$i$ contributes little to $j$'s.

The broader contribution is to make the interaction structure in a spatial
finance model an object of measurement.
Spatial asset-pricing models require a matrix $W$ describing which firms
interact and with what weight.
Rather than specifying this structure from geography, industry
classifications, or the returns subsequently used for evaluation, we measure it
from an earlier information environment and hold it fixed.
This distinction matters because economically relevant information about firms,
such as their products, customers, suppliers, technologies, and risks, can
accumulate independently of the history of return co-movement from which
cross-firm dependence would otherwise be inferred.
Subsequent returns therefore evaluate the financial relevance of the measured
field rather than determine its construction.

We study \ppvalue{n-tickers-priced} continuously covered large-cap firms
traded in the United States from a Nasdaq-100-based universe.
We construct the field from 2018--2022 news, hold it fixed, and evaluate it on
\ppvalue{transport-evaluation-trading-days} trading days in 2023--2026, with
2026 covering a partial year.
For the held-out exercise, all return-model parameters are fitted on
2018--2022 returns before the later observations are scored.
A spatial factor model connects the field to returns, and a quadratic
exposure-adjustment problem, in which a firm balances its stand-alone exposure
against alignment with its information peers, gives the spatial coefficient an
economic interpretation.
With unrestricted firm coefficients, reduced-form factor betas remain exactly
equal to firm-by-firm least-squares estimates.
Standard factors therefore continue to describe exposure to common systematic
risks, and the field's contribution lies in residual covariance.

The frozen field improves held-out density scores relative to a matched model
with the Fama--French five factors and momentum, conditional on realized factor
returns, with paired intervals above zero.
Joint representation is the source of the gain: pairwise Wasserstein
proximity captures less than a third of it, and equal weighting of the selected
peers captures less than half.
Centroid reconstruction retains most of the gain, while the full distribution
adds a smaller, separately measurable increment.
Explicit news co-mentions capture a complementary dimension of relatedness, and
each field improves held-out joint density conditional on the other.
A conventional residual-covariance estimator has a higher held-out point
score, although the paired difference is statistically uncertain; the
contribution is an interpretable peer structure whose financial relevance can
be evaluated out of period.
The reconstruction advantage is stable across transport costs and, in sample,
across alternative embedding models.
The primary embedding model was released after the news window, so the
representation measures earlier news retrospectively.
We assess sensitivity to this retrospective measurement with an encoder
whose reported training cutoff precedes the article window.
