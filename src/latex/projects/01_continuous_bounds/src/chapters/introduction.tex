\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Factor models represent systematic return variation through exposures to
common shocks, and the resulting covariances determine how firm risks
aggregate in a portfolio
\citep{ross_arbitrage_1976,fama_multifactor_1996,daniel_evidence_1997}.
The recent wave of large initial public offerings makes this dependence
question especially consequential: a prospective holding can have substantial
economic importance and little public trading history, alongside years of
reporting about its products, customers, suppliers, and contracts.
Nasdaq's record first-half listings in 2026 illustrate the scale of this
information mismatch \citep{nasdaq_listings_2026}.
An investment manager needs to understand how those activities relate to risks
already present in the portfolio, including connections obscured by sector
labels or diversified business models.

Language models make this documentary evidence increasingly accessible.
\citet{lopezlira_chatgpt_2026} document that headline assessments capture
initial stock-price responses and predict subsequent drift.
Assessing an individual company's news raises a further portfolio question:
how do its risks fit together with those of other holdings?
The characteristics used to describe factor exposures are usually encoded as
points or finite vectors.
Yet evidence about a firm arrives as a collection of reports about its
products, costs, contracts, and markets.
Retaining that collection as a distribution preserves heterogeneity within
the firm and raises a financial question: what does separation between two
such distributions imply for the systematic risk the firms can share?

We derive a covariance restriction from the geometry of those distributions.
Quadratic Wasserstein distance, denoted W$_2$, measures the minimum
root-mean-square displacement needed to align their probability mass.
A coupling specifies which draws from the two distributions occur together.
In factor-exposure space, the coupling with the smallest squared displacement
attains the greatest systematic covariance.
This follows from polarization: covariance equals average exposure magnitudes
minus half the expected squared distance between exposures.
The distance between the marginal exposure laws therefore determines a sharp
covariance ceiling; the realized coupling falls below it by half its excess
transport cost.

To connect that result to firm information, we introduce a common benchmark
relationship between economic characteristics and factor exposures, allowing
bounded firm-specific departures.
The benchmark describes how activities and contracts transmit common shocks
to equity returns: fuel intensity, fare flexibility, hedging, and leverage,
for example, shape an airline's exposure to fuel prices.
Bounds on the distortion of characteristic distances and on departures from
the benchmark yield an interval for the covariance ceiling.

Empirically, we represent each firm by a distribution of language-model
embeddings of financial news, disclosures, and analyst reports for a Nasdaq
sample spanning \ppvalue{sample-year-start}--\ppvalue{sample-year-end}.
News production gives this representation economic content: investors demand
information about cash flows and risks, while reporting costs and audience
interest influence which activities receive coverage.
The empirical design explains this selection mechanism and relates distances
between the resulting article distributions to return co-movement.

In a dyadic regression with symmetric firm effects, one standard deviation
more article-embedding W$_2$ corresponds to roughly eight fewer correlation points.
The positive return-distance association persists after sector and finer
industry matching, within industries, and after controlling for
Hoberg--Phillips 10-K product-market similarity.
It also retains its sign when returns are measured in a later window.
A separate sensitivity grid evaluates the covariance restriction over
specified transmission scenarios.
Its informativeness declines rapidly as multiplicative distortion and
firm-specific slack increase, showing how tightly the transmission
relationship must be specified for the restriction to discriminate among
candidate covariances.

Controlled comparisons across encoders, embedding widths, distance costs,
and reported training-information vintages examine how the representation
measures this relationship.
Together with transport's allocation and adjustment-cost interpretations,
they connect the covariance argument to economically interpretable
measurement choices.

The contribution connects a distributional representation of firm information,
a sharp covariance result, and evidence on return co-movement.
For practitioners, article distance supplies a way to screen firm pairs for
shared risks across conventional classifications.
The covariance envelope then organizes an assessment of those pairs under
explicit assumptions about how their economic characteristics translate into
systematic exposures.
